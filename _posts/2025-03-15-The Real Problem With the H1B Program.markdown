I'm a little late to the party here, but I've certainly had lots of offline conversations with friends and former colleagues re the H1B kerfuffle over in MAGA world (see here for example). While the world has by now largely moved on to apoplexy over Trump's minute-by-minute edicts on tariffs, this issue remains an important one, especially as big tech is now embracing MAGA in a way few of us (due to wishful thinking, maybe?) could have imagined the first time around.

So, without further ado...the real issue I saw with the H1B program while working at a Fortune 5 company that extensively relies on H1B workers to staff its IT roles is that it's seemingly designed to give American employers an unhealthy (and undemocratic) amount of leverage over their workers (foreign _and_ domestic). And of course, this has repercussions for domestic technology workers as well. I would argue that it in fact has consequences on a broader societal level, given how utterly dependent American big business is upon IT workers, even if technology happens not to be a firm's primary focus.

One thing which I was surprised to learn from H1B colleagues was that their visas are not really theirs. They don’t own them, in any real sense. They belong, instead, to their employer. One practical consequence of this is that it becomes very difficult for H1B workers to “vote with their feet”, by leaving organizations that don’t treat them well. It also has the effect of depressing wages for H1B workers, and - I suspect - for their native colleagues. 

An H1B worker may have lived and worked in the US for years, perhaps decades, and will still be beholden to the whims of the employer that sponsors them. They may have gotten married here, their children may have been born here, and they may own property here in the US (not to mention the taxes they pay - including those paid into social security, a program whose benefits they may not ever be eligible to receive) all without the legal protections afforded by full citizenship. And should they ever _lose_ their positions, they have - generally - six short months to secure a new position with another sponsoring employer. If they are unsuccessful, they may have to leave the country or pay a heavy fine until they can secure sponsorship through another sponsoring employer. 

This means that H1B workers don’t get to negotiate their employment in the US on a level playing field. The risks of losing a position are dire, and can easily lead to having to uproot one’s entire family (including children who may have only ever lived in or known the US). This gives employers an inordinate amount of leverage over these employees. And from what I’ve personally seen, this is leverage that employers are more than happy to use to extract more work for less pay from H1B workers. 

So if you support the H1B in its current form - it’s _actual_ form - like comrade Ramaswamy does, then what you’re basically supporting is the importation of less expensive foreign workers who will accept substandard wages and tolerate a never ending “rat race” mentality because things are even _worse_ where they came from. And that acceptance is fundamentally predicated upon a piece of legal chicanery - the H1B - which ensures that those workers, no matter how hard they work, or for how long, will enjoy a subordinate status to domestic workers _even as the work they do displaces and undercuts those same domestic tech workers. 

Musk, to his credit, did give voice to the obvious solution to the above problem: Ensure that H1Bs are somehow taxed so as to make it too costly for domestic roles to fall victim to wage arbitrage unless there _really is_ no other choice than to import a worker to fill a particular role. But I wouldn’t hold my breath waiting for this reform to be passed, especially while comrade Musk is busy stripping veterans of their military benefits. 

Until next time.
