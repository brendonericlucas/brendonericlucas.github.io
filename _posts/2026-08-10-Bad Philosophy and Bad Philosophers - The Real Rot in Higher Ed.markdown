# It's Been A Minute

Well, more like one year and five months, give or take.

In the time since I've last contributed a post I've been a little bit busy with a PhD program in physics and (much more importantly) with participating in the bringing into existence of one very lovely and delightful baby girl (Hi, Willow!) who - I hope - has not been harmed at all by the aforementioned process (I'm looking at you, [David Benetar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Benatar)).

This is a bit of an odd topic to return on, but I got sucked into this last evening during a bout of insomnia and - frankly - I just couldn't resist.

Evidently, [this guy](https://nathancofnas.com/), who describes himself as a philosopher working at the intersection of philosophy of biology "very broadly construed" and ethics, and whose interests are in

> "scientific and ethical controversies connected with evolution-informed social science."

has [outed a Cambridge sociologist for suspected plagiarism](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/08/08/opinion/cambridge-plagiarism-arday-higher-education.html).

Earlier accustaions of plagiarism dating back to 2018 (not made by the philosopher whose work occasioned this blog post) can be found [here](https://www.arasite.org/Ardaypage.html).

I would encourage anyone with even a mild interest in these issues to *actually read* the author of that page's charges against Ardy. I did. Upon doing so, I was immediately reminded of this piece in the NYT from John McWhorter[^1], which contains this salient thought:

>"To present someone else’s ideas as one’s own is unquestionably wrong, in academia and elsewhere. However, to cite boilerplate statements — the assumptions basic to a field, for instance — word for word, or close to it, without citing the person who typed the words originally is something different, and vastly less egregious. I would argue, in fact, that there may be nothing wrong with it at all, in particular when it is done accidentally."

McWhorter was writing against the backdrop of the Claudine Gay plagiarism scandal there, partly in defense of Harvard's choice to label the offenses of which Gay had been accused instances of "duplicative language", a linguistic innovation gesturing at a level of offense that perhaps implicates one as having some troublingly "sloppy" scholarly habits which nevertheless do not rise to anywhere near the level of plagiarism in the specific sense of "[presenting] someone else's ideas as one's own".

McWhorter, for the record, came down on the side of Gay's resignation being the correct call, the reason being that the *official* definition of plagiarism at Harvard at the time *did not* make the distinction between "full-blooded plagiarism" and the less egregious offense of having been guilty of using "duplicative language".

McWhorter again:

>"But here in this world, in this language, the term “plagiarism” still covers both “real” plagiarism — the theft of another person’s ideas — and the use, perhaps inadvertent, of another person’s language. For that reason, I continue to think Gay was correct to step down — especially given that Harvard, like many universities, explicitly defines plagiarism for undergraduates in the “old” way. If accidental cutting and pasting could theoretically get a sophomore suspended, repeated instances of the same should lead an administrator to step down."

I think McWhorter is correct on both counts. Given our current "crude" conception of "plagiarism" - and the likely consequences for students and junior scholars of having been found guilty of the offense - Gay needed to resign (and so she did). But that conception is also, again, crude and without nuance and likely needs to be refined in exactly the way that McWhorter suggests.[^2]

The other issue lurking in the background here - and I think this is implicit in McWhorter's framing, whether he realizes it or not - is that *class* is often the more salient factor in deciding how issues play out in academic contexts, at least in the United States. Gay is black, and a women, both of which characteristics might be presumed to put her at a relative disadvantage knowing nothing more about her.[^3] But we *do* know more about her. She was at the time the president of the wealthiest university in the world and a person of some significant economic privilege due to the wealth of her extended family as well as her own salary as the ranking administrator and an academic at Harvard. Cultural privilege and some level of social privlege tend to accrue to people with Gay's education and professional background as well. Implicit in McWhorter's "if a sophmore could theoretically get suspended" remark, then, is a very reasonable insistence that economic, cultural, and social privilege not be allowed to create a tiered set of standards for acceptable academic conduct which allows the powerful and influential to escape the consequences of violating said standards in virtue of their class. This principle is worth adhering to, I think, even if the specific standards being enforced in particular cases are not wholly in good order and require some adjustment. A society cannot have genuine accountability without having elite accountability.

Which brings us back to the Ardy affair. The philosopher who brought the claims against Ardy is himself a former Cambridge academic (more on that below), who calls himself a "race realist" and has produced  some absolute gems, including some outside of his published scholarly output, such as [this essay on SubStack](https://ncofnas.com/p/are-smart-people-superior-a-reply) where he defends the idea that more intelligent people are of inherently greater worth than their less intelligent peers.

Here are a few passages that caught my attention...

> "We also blame people for manifesting their natural stupidity by choosing to do something offensive—for example, arguing about something they don’t understand, or occupying a position of influence for which they are not qualified. Thus, we might call a low-IQ Internet commenter, an incompetent politician, or a not-so-bright professor an “idiot” in an insulting way. **This can be justified on the grounds that the offending person made the choice to strive for something for which they had no natural right.** There’s nothing blameworthy per se about the Jacob Urowsky Professor of Philosophy at Yale Jason Stanley getting an SAT score of 1280, which is more than a standard deviation above average. We insult Stanley for his intelligence when he poses as a great intellect with the authority to pass judgment on everyone else."

The bolded clause is the most salient for present purposes, though it's probably also worth noting the (weirdly hedging)[^4] personal slight against Stanley. Personal attacks of this sort are, famously, a surefire indication of a superior intellect!

Having already declared himself some sort of moral anti-realist earlier on in the essay, the reference to one having "no natural right" is a bit curious. Speaking in terms of natural rights typically puts one squarely in the moral realist camp, but I suppose this could just be some rhetorical sloppiness. Still, we'll come back to this later. 

The next passage that caught my attention occurs at a point where the author seems to be trying to engage in some positive theorizing: 

>"There is no formula for calculating “human worth,” and no objective standard that tells us how to weigh different values. But I suggest the following general principle holds: **all else being equal, more of a worth-giving property (whether it has intrinsic and/or instrumental value) imparts greater aggregate worth.**"

Wow! What a move! A simple additive model of human worth. Of course! There couldn't *possibly* be any obvious, plausible, seeming counterexamples to such a model.

One couldn't, for example, simply imagine a world in which there are only two worth-giving properties, *charm* and *beauty*, which together determine an individual's "aggregate worth". 

And since *that* is impossible to imagine, it's equally impossible to imagine that there is some non-trivial (i.e., not purely additive) interaction between those two properties such that - beyond a certain point - the more you have of one of them, the less you have of the other.

For example, one simply cannot imagine that being more beautiful only has an additive effect on one's aggregate worth *up to* some threshold, beyond which - say - one's *charm* begins to diminish and to diminish in such a way that the effect of one's loss of charm more than outweighs the effect of one's gain of beauty for purposes of tabulating their aggregate worth. It is impossible to conceive, for example, that the development of charm may, as a matter of fact, occur primarily as an adaptive response to perceived deficits in one's beauty, as a means of compensating for those perceived deficits and that once one possesses beauty beyond a certain threshold, one no longer bothers with the cultivation of charm.

That is, one simply *cannot conceive* of a scalar model that is a function of two factors and which is *not* just an unbounded (or bounded), non-negative increasing function of those two factors. The impossibility here is a mathematical one, clearly, as well as one of the imaginative variety.

Sarcasm aside, I see no reason to believe that the bolded claim is true. Everything I take myself to know about ordinary, everday estimations of the worth of individuals suggests that assessments of worth are much more complicated and nuanced than the additive model the author proposes suggests, that different worth-conferring properties can - and do - interact, and that at least some of those interations are probably also causal.

One could, I suppose, retreat into the *ceteris paribus* rider. This is a common move made by many philosophers who are attracted to less rigorous ways of attempting to tackle problems. The danger, of course, is that if it is interpreted *too broadly*, then it reduces the claim above to, essentially, **more of a worth-giving property (whether it has intrinsic and/or instrumental value) imparts greater aggregate worth, except inasmuch as it doesn't.** That is trivially true, of course, but for that very reason also totally uninformative and completely uninteresting.

But *why* am I harping on this? Well, for one thing, if you replace "beauty" with "intelligence" in the example above, it immediately undermines the basis for the author's claim. 

There is, however, an even more important point to be made here. The bolded claim is an example of *bad philosophy*. The theorizing it is intended to support is *bad theorizing*. It is a manifestly *dumb* claim to make, in that it suffers from many immediate and obvious counterexamples, is not psychologically plausible, and purchases a "clean statement" of a general principle at the expense of rendinering the principle applicable to nothing (or to everything, which amounts to the same thing). 

And it is being presented in the context of an essay which argues for the inherently greater "human worth" of individuals with "greater intelligence". It therefore demonstrates, among other things, a *startling* lack of self-awareness. 

The author, it seems, is incapable of entertaining that - when he writes something like this

> "We also blame people for manifesting their natural stupidity by choosing to...strive for something for which they had no natural right."

his audience may in fact include himself.

The essay I pulled from above, to be sure, was written long before the author involved himself in the Ardy affair. But I think it's important for framing what's going on with Ardy right now, and - pace the NYT article on this[^5] - for understanding the *real* issues in higher ed, especially in the USA.

The philosopher who outed Ardy, it should be said, has *impeccable* educational credentials: a B.A. from Columbia, an MPhil from Cambridge (with distinction) and a PhD from Oxford (also, weirdly, a five year stint at a university in Hong Kong which does not seem to have resulted in a degree being conferred).[^6] He was himself a Philosophy Fellow at Emmanuel College, Cambridge, until the university severed ties with him - he claims - over his "race realist" views.[^7]

In certain respects, the author reminds me of one of Peter Turchin's [frustrated elite aspirants](https://democracyparadox.com/2023/06/13/peter-turchin-wants-to-avoid-political-disintegration/). I would not personally rate the author's work very highly (take that for what you will), and I suspect that he may not be the most pleasant person to have around. 

The latter may seem of secondary importance, but full-time academic jobs often come with robust protections. It can be very difficult for a department to rid itself of a "false positive" hire, and much more costly than missing out on a good candidate. Knowing that hiring someone means you may be stuck working next to them for the next 30+ years has a way of changing the calculus for hiring decisions in a way that favors a certain conservatism and risk aversion. I don't know - and can't say - if that's part of what happened to the author, but there's a certain "vibe" in his work output which suggests to me that it isn't unreasonable to think it might well be.

The author's *intellectual project* also seems suspect to me, in the specific sense that I'm just not really sure what it's supposed to be *for*. Suppose that the author were to actually succeed in persuading the majority of people that differences in the average IQ across racial groups had a *causal* relationship to race, including perhaps members of the races the author himself believes to be "less worthy". Then what, exactly?

The standard next move for people who hold these views is to then try to argue that *it is a waste of society's resources* to support DEI initiatives, since the broader societal changes the initiatives are ostensibly aiming at *just aren't capable* of moving the needles that need to be moved, here. It is inborn traits - like intelligence - that are responsible for differences in things like life and career outcomes, and those are due to heritable factors, are determined at birth, and so cannot ever be changed. 

Several thoughts here. Firstly, the above presents as clear-cut what is arguably still a very murky area within the scientific study of human intelligence. No one knows with confidence how much of the variation in individual intelligence can be accounted for based purely on heredity, though "typical" estimates range between 50% and 80%. But there are good reasons to tread cautiously here. For one thing, intelligence is surely a polygenetic trait[^8], and the more polygenetic a trait is the more challenging it is (in general) to understand its genetic basis. 

Not only this, but there are also issues with using proxy measures for intelligence (e.g., SAT scores) in countries like the US, where family wealth and income correlate as well as - or better - with standardized test scores than race does and where, for historical reasons, a large racial wealth gap exists.

Some studies also suggest that the influence of heredity on intelligence may not be uniform over the course of an individual's lifetime[^9], and that early life heritability may be as low as 20%. This obviously raises additional questions about whether the allocation of scarce resources by society to improve life outcomes may be more impactful if it occurs earlier in life rather than later.

The point is, the question of what exactly the science on the relationship between race and heredity and intelligence holds is *complicated*, and genuinely unclear.

But there is a more direct answer to that "next turn" people like the author tend to want to make. It comes in the form of a question: "So what?"

So what *if* it turns out to be true that race largely determines intelligence? So what *if* it turns out to be true that some races are more (conventionally) intelligent than others, and that the realtionship between race and average intelligence is causal? So what?

The *turn* only follows if we assume that the only legitimate way of pursuing the goals of DEI is to move the intelligence needle. But why should that be the case? Certainly, the most direct way to positively alter indivudal life outcomes (including career outcomes) is to simply *positively alter individuals' life outcomes*. This doesn't require moving the intelligence needle unless the differences in intelligence *in particular cases* are such as would tend to cause actual harm - or at least make it more likely to occur - to the individuals benefited themselves *or* to other individuals. But I don't see any indication in many cases that this is so, unless the alleged "harm" is that incurred to people like the author when they aren't able to secure the positions (in acadamia, in politics, in elite corporate positions, etc.) which they imagine are *simply their due*.

I suspect that the latter is in fact the case. The author believes that *they have been personally harmed* by DEI initiatives because the limited number of seats at certain elite tables are now occupied by individuals who might not have had a shot at occupying those seats without the benefits and advantages conferred by DEI policies that the author himself is not able to leverage in his own struggle for elite status. So we're back to the Peter Turchin / "frustrated elite aspirant" framing. 

On this view, the motivation for "race realism" and similar such views is the desire of frustrated elite aspirants to claim a place at an elite table by *displacing* those from less traditional backgrounds who have made it to the table ahead of them, and - in some cases - instead of them. 

One of the characteristics of Turchin's frustrated elites is that they often turn to strategies like "ethnic entrepreneurialism", stirring up resentment and animus between members of different ethinc groups to create social and political pressure they hope to then be able to leverage to force a seat at the table. The tradgedy for the "ordinary folk" who support them is that - even if the frustrated elites win their place - not much tends to change for those who suported them. *They* don't win a seat at the table, and in fact none was ever even on offer (I'm looking at you, J.D. Vance).

It helps to consider what society would presumably get in exchange for repealing the DEI policies that may help some scholars from certain backgrounds sercure a place at the table.

In this case, if the author had his way, it would presumably get a bill the paying of which would secure one philospher's continued ability to pursue his intellectual project of convinving the world that some races are inherently more "worthy" than others. Golly gee! What a great ROI on public invstment *that* would be. 

I feel for Ardy, and I understand his decision to step away. Whether he is guilty of plagiarism or the less egregious offense of "duplicative language", he is being treated terribly by the author and others in his orbit. And I suspect that if it had not been *the author* who surfaced these charges, Cambridge might have continued to support Ardy the way it had long done. It would surprise me if the university hadn't received legal counsel to the effect that it couldn't afford to support Ardy without an internal investigation given the pending litigation between the author and itself. And it doesn't surprise me that Ardy might have interpreted that as a loss of insitutional support which - combined with the unwanted media attention - made his continuing in his position untenable.

I would submit to the author of the NYT article that - especially in the US - dissatisfaction with higher ed has much less to do with the decision of institutions to stand behind and stand up for scholars like Ardy, and much more to do with the way that our institutions have allowed themselves to become "the machines of inequality" all the while lecturing the rest of us on how we need to practice greater inclusivity and tolerance. That is, higher ed's crisis of public trust isn't primarily about institutions being too protective of scholars like Ardy; it's about institutions functioning as engines of stratification while performing egalitarian rhetoric. The Ardy affair, to me, perfectly illustrates this because *it's being weaponized by someone whose intellectual project is itself a bid for elite positioning dressed up as truth-telling*. And it is, moreover, a contest *between* elites over who gets to be one (Ardy, despite his humble origins, unquestionably occupied a position of elite privilege while at Cambridge); both the accuser and the accused are players in an elite-status game, and the public's disillusionment with higher ed reflects a correct intuition that a lot of what happens inside these institutions is *that game* rather than the pursuit of knowledge or public benefit.

In short, people no longer know what academia is *for*, and that as often as not includes academics themselves.
